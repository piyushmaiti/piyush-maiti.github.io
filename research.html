Research & Publications

Physics, Signals, Disease and AI

My work sits at the intersection of imaging physics, signal processing and machine learning, with a focus on making quantitative brain imaging reliable. At the Imaging Genetics Center at USC, developing diffusion MRI pipelines for ADNI showed me how differences in scanner hardware, acquisition protocols and reconstruction affect not only image contrast but also the methods we use to analyze it, which led me to help develop a style-encoding GAN for multisite MRI harmonization that preserves anatomy. At Washington University, I investigated how the scanner's point-spread function causes signal to spill across anatomical boundaries in preclinical PET, and developed a geometry-adaptive sampling method to reduce partial-volume bias. At UCSF's Memory and Aging Center, I led MRI-based PET processing and designed the quality-control system for the multisite LEADS study, where the measures I generated informed clinical decisions and the evaluation of patients receiving anti-amyloid therapy. Across these projects, I have learned that a measurement is only as reliable as our understanding of how it was acquired, processed and validated. That understanding guides my current work building clinical AI tools at Whissle.ai and my independent research on ultrashort echo time MRI of myelin, and reflects my long-term goal to engineer robust and mathematically principled imaging systems for research and precision diagnostics.

3D Skeleton-Based PET Quantification

In development

Small mouse-brain regions read too low in PET because of the scanner's limited resolution and small registration errors. I sample each region along its 3D skeleton, inflated with spheres sized to the local thickness, so the measurement stays away from the region's edge and needs no scanner model. Validated on digital phantoms with known activity.

Style-Transfer GAN for Multi-site MRI Harmonization

MICCAI 2021

MRI scans pooled from many sites differ in contrast and intensity because of the scanner, not the brain. We treat harmonization as style transfer: a GAN based on StarGAN v2 takes the scanner "style" from a single reference scan and applies it to any brain while keeping the anatomy, with no site labels or traveling subjects. Trained on 718 T1-weighted scans from five cohorts (UK Biobank, ADNI, PPMI, ABCD, ICBM); it raised segmentation Dice on an unseen dataset and worked on unseen scanners.
